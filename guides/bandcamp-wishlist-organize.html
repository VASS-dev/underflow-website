Bandcamp wishlist: how to organize it and turn it into a buy list

A wishlist is easy to fill and hard to use. After a few months it holds hundreds of items with no order, no notes and no difference between "interesting" and "buy this week". Here is a workflow that keeps it useful.

Why a wishlist stops working

Saving a release takes one click, so you save everything. What is missing is the second step: deciding. Without a way to mark what is for a specific set, what is a maybe and what is already decided, the list becomes a backlog you never open.

A three-stage workflow

  1. Wishlist = maybe. Anything that sounded good. No pressure to buy.
  2. Buy list = decided. Review the wishlist on a fixed day, move the tracks you are sure about, and drop the rest.
  3. Collection = bought. Move items once purchased, so you never pay twice for the same track.

Whatever tool you use, keep the stages separate. The point is that "I like this" and "I will pay for this" are different decisions.

Tags that match how you play

Tag by what you will do with the track, not only by genre: a set name, a mood, a tempo range, a key. Genre is already in the store; your own tags are the part nobody else can give you.

Community tools

People have built tools around Bandcamp's collection pages. Two we found: the userscript Bandcamp Collection Filters (adds filtering), and bandcamp-collection-sorter on GitHub, which reorders a collection using Last.fm listening data and, per its description, overwrites the previous order when run. We have not tested either, so read their pages and make a backup before using anything that changes your collection.

Doing this across several stores

If you also buy on Beatport, Juno Records or Decks.de, a Bandcamp-only wishlist cannot hold the whole list. In Underflow you heart a track from any of the five stores, move it to a buy list, and mark it as owned once purchased. Your tags, wishlists and collection live in a local SQLite database on your own machine and can be exported to CSV.
