Where do DJs buy music in 2026? Stores, pools and streaming

There is no single place DJs buy music. The sources fall into a few groups, and each one is good at something different. This guide maps them, based on the 2026 updates of two long-running Digital DJ Tips guides.

Mainstream download stores

Amazon and the iTunes Store are the easy option. Digital DJ Tips says Amazon is simple to buy from and cheap, with most music available, but it will not carry the most underground or independent tracks, and its files are often 256 kbps MP3s; the same article recommends 320 kbps or lossless for DJing. The iTunes Store has a wide selection at competitive prices, but it uses AAC rather than MP3, and the article warns about mixing bought music with Apple Music.

Specialist download stores

  • Beatport is the main store for electronic dance music. It carries most underground labels, has charts and a preview player, and still runs a large download store next to its streaming push.
  • Traxsource (founded in 2004) is mainly underground house, with techno, progressive house and nu-disco. It sells AIFF, WAV and MP3 and has store-compiled and DJ-compiled charts. See Traxsource vs Beatport.
  • Volumo focuses on underground electronic music (house, techno, drum & bass, breaks). It is curated, competitively priced and has no extra charge for lossless formats.
  • Boomkat leans underground and alternative (dub techno, dark ambient, grime, industrial, jazz), selling vinyl and CDs plus MP3 and FLAC downloads.
  • Juno Download closed on 1 June 2026. Its vinyl-and-gear sibling still trades; see Juno Records vs Juno Download.

Bandcamp

Bandcamp is artist-focused: artists set their own prices and, per Digital DJ Tips, keep 82% of revenue. The same article stresses that it is not built for DJs, and its 2026 update says few DJs use it as a primary source. It is where you find small labels and music that never reaches the big stores. See Bandcamp vs Beatport for DJs.

Record pools

Pools are monthly subscriptions. Digital DJ Tips describes them as more cost-effective than buying tracks one by one, with edits and versions the public cannot buy, but best for commercial music and with little or no older music. See record pool vs buying tracks.

Streaming

Streaming services are built into DJ software and hardware, which makes them good for practice and casual sets. The same guide warns about technical limits, and that you depend on a subscription and the service's label licences to keep access, so it recommends owning your core library. See streaming vs owning.

Physical records

Vinyl and CDs cover older music that is not on streaming. Digital DJ Tips notes that old compilation CDs are much cheaper per track than downloads (you need a CD ripper), that Discogs is a source of new and used copies, and that buying vinyl only to rip it should be avoided where possible, since many releases include a digital download voucher.

The real problem: it is all in different places

If you dig for one artist, the answer might be on Bandcamp, Beatport, a vinyl-only release on Juno Records or Decks.de, or a used copy on Discogs.
